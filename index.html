NCERT Section 14.2 • Part 1

Classification of Metals, Conductors and Semiconductors

Classification on the basis of electrical conductivity (σ) or resistivity (ρ = 1/σ), plus Elemental and Compound Semiconductors.

Concept Explorer • On the Basis of Conductivity

On the basis of the relative values of electrical conductivity (σ) or resistivity (ρ = 1/σ), solids are broadly classified into three categories:

Reciprocal Relationship
ρ = 1σ   or   σ = 1ρ
ρ (Resistivity): SI Unit = Ω m (ohm-metre)
σ (Conductivity): SI Unit = S m−1 or Ω−1 m−1
Dimensions of ρ: [M1 L3 T−3 A−2]
(i) METALS (Conductors)

Possess very low resistivity (or high conductivity).

ρ ∼ 10−2 – 10−8 Ω m
σ ∼ 102 – 108 S m−1
(ii) SEMICONDUCTORS

Have resistivity or conductivity intermediate to metals and insulators.

ρ ∼ 10−5 – 106 Ω m
σ ∼ 105 – 10−6 S m−1
(iii) INSULATORS

Have high resistivity (or low conductivity).

ρ ∼ 1011 – 1019 Ω m
σ ∼ 10−11 – 10−19 S m−1
Important NCERT Observation: The values of ρ and σ given above are indicative of magnitude and could well go outside the ranges as well. Relative values of resistivity are not the only criteria for distinguishing metals, insulators, and semiconductors from each other (for instance, their temperature dependence and carrier concentrations also differ fundamentally).
Types of Semiconductors in NCERT

Our interest in this chapter is in the study of semiconductors, which are classified by chemical composition into:

(i) Elemental Semiconductors Group 14

Single-element crystals: Silicon (Si) and Germanium (Ge).

(ii) Compound Semiconductors 3 Sub-classes
• Inorganic: CdS, GaAs, CdSe, InP, etc.
• Organic: anthracene, doped pthalocyanines, etc.
• Organic polymers: polypyrrole, polyaniline, polythiophene, etc.
Evolution Beyond 1990 (Polymer & Molecular Electronics)

Most currently available semiconductor devices are based on elemental semiconductors (Si or Ge) and compound inorganic semiconductors. However, after 1990, semiconductor devices using organic semiconductors and semiconducting polymers were developed, signalling the birth of polymer-electronics and molecular-electronics (used today in flexible OLED displays and wearable sensors). In this chapter, we focus on inorganic elemental semiconductors (Si and Ge).

NCERT Section 14.2 • Part 2 (Pages 325–327)

Classification on the Basis of Energy Bands

From isolated Bohr atoms to 8N crystal energy states, Valence Band (EV), Conduction Band (EC), Energy Gap (Eg), and Figures 14.1 & 14.2.

Concept Explorer • How Energy Bands Form in a Crystal

According to the Bohr atomic model, in an isolated atom the energy of any of its electrons is decided by the orbit in which it revolves. But when the atoms come together to form a solid they are close to each other. So the outer orbits of electrons from neighbouring atoms come very close or could even overlap. This makes the nature of electron motion in a solid very different from that in an isolated atom.

Inside the crystal each electron has a unique position and no two electrons see exactly the same pattern of surrounding charges. Because of this, each electron will have a different energy level. These different energy levels with continuous energy variation form what are called energy bands:

1. Valence Band (Highest level: EV)

The energy band which includes the energy levels of the valence electrons is called the valence band. With no external energy (at T = 0 K), all valence electrons reside in the valence band.

2. Conduction Band (Lowest level: EC)

The energy band above the valence band is called the conduction band. Normally the conduction band is empty at 0 K (in semiconductors and insulators), unless it overlaps the valence band (in metals).

8N Quantitative State Counting in a Si or Ge Crystal of N Atoms

  • 1. For Silicon (Si, Z = 14), the outermost orbit is the third orbit (n = 3: 3s2 3p2), while for Germanium (Ge, Z = 32) it is the fourth orbit (n = 4: 4s2 4p2).
  • 2. The number of outer electrons per atom is 4 (2s and 2p electrons). Hence, the total number of outer (valence) electrons in a crystal of N atoms is 4N.
  • 3. The maximum possible number of electrons in the outer orbit is 8 (2s + 6p electrons). So, for the 4N valence electrons there are 8N available energy states.
  • 4. At the crystal lattice spacing of Si and Ge, the energy band of these 8N states is split apart into two bands separated by an energy gap Eg:
    • Lower band = Valence Band (4N energy states, completely occupied by the 4N valence electrons at 0 K).
    • Upper band = Conduction Band (4N energy states, completely empty at 0 K).

CASE I: Fig. 14.2(a) Eg ≈ 0

Metallic Conductors

One can have a metal in two situations:
(i) When the conduction band is partially filled and the valence band is partially empty, or
(ii) When the conduction and valence bands overlap (Eg ≈ 0).

Consequence: Electrons from the valence band easily move into the conduction band (or higher levels in a partially empty band), making a huge number of electrons available for electrical conduction. Resistance is very low; conductivity is high.

CASE II: Fig. 14.2(b) Eg > 3 eV

Insulators (Large Band Gap)

A large band gap Eg exists (Eg > 3 eV, e.g., Diamond Eg ≈ 5.4 eV). There are no electrons in the conduction band.

Consequence: The energy gap is so large that electrons cannot be excited from the valence band to the conduction band by thermal excitation at room temperature (kBT ≈ 0.026 eV). Hence no electrical conduction is possible.

CASE III: Fig. 14.2(c) Eg < 3 eV

Semiconductors (Small Band Gap)

A finite but small band gap (Eg < 3 eV) exists (e.g., Si: 1.1 eV, Ge: 0.72 eV).

Consequence: Because of the small band gap, at room temperature (T > 0 K) some electrons from the valence band acquire enough thermal energy to cross Eg and enter the conduction band, while leaving behind vacancies (holes) in the valence band.

Intrinsic Semiconductors, Hole Motion & Dual-Carrier Conduction

Diamond-like crystal structure (Fig. 14.3), covalent bonds (Fig. 14.4), hole generation & apparent hole motion from Site 1 to Site 2 (Fig. 14.5), ne = nh = ni, and I = Ie + Ih.

Concept Explorer • Diamond-Like Lattice & Covalent Bonds

We take the most common case of Germanium (Ge) and Silicon (Si) whose lattice structure is shown in Fig. 14.3. These structures are called the diamond-like structures. Each atom is surrounded by four nearest neighbours at the vertices of a regular tetrahedron.

Si and Ge have four valence electrons. In its crystalline structure, every Si or Ge atom tends to share one of its four valence electrons with each of its four nearest neighbour atoms, and also to take share of one electron from each such neighbour. These shared electron pairs are referred to as forming a covalent bond (or simply a valence bond). The two shared electrons can be assumed to shuttle back-and-forth between the associated atoms holding them together strongly.

Thermal Bond Breaking, Holes (+q) & Intrinsic Carrier Concentration

At low temperatures, all covalent bonds are intact (Fig. 14.4). As the temperature increases, more thermal energy becomes available to these electrons and some of these electrons may break away (becoming free electrons contributing to conduction). The thermal energy effectively ionises only a few atoms in the crystalline lattice and creates a vacancy in the bond as shown in Fig. 14.5(a).

The neighbourhood, from which the free electron (with charge −q) has come out leaves a vacancy with an effective charge (+q). This vacancy with the effective positive electronic charge is called a hole. The hole behaves as an apparent free particle with effective positive charge.

INTRINSIC CARRIER CONCENTRATION Eq. (14.1)
ne = nh = ni

Where ne = free electron density, nh = hole density, and ni = intrinsic carrier concentration (SI unit: m−3).

TOTAL SEMICONDUCTOR CURRENT Eq. (14.2)
I = Ie + Ih

Total current I under an applied electric field is the sum of free electron current Ie and bound-electron hole current Ih (SI unit: Ampere, A).

Dynamic Equilibrium: Generation vs Recombination

Apart from the process of generation of conduction electrons and holes, a simultaneous process of recombination occurs in which the electrons recombine with the holes (when an electron collides with a hole). At thermal equilibrium: Rate of Generation = Rate of Recombination

Worked Examples & Systematic Problem Solving
NCERT EXAMPLE 14.1 (Page 329) Conceptual Reasoning

Question: C, Si and Ge have same lattice structure. Why is C insulator while Si and Ge intrinsic semiconductors?

Solution (Exact NCERT Logic):

The 4 bonding electrons of C, Si or Ge lie, respectively, in the second (n = 2), third (n = 3) and fourth (n = 4) orbit.

Hence, energy required to take out an electron from these atoms (i.e., ionisation energy Eg) will be least for Ge (≈ 0.72 eV), followed by Si (≈ 1.1 eV) and highest for C (≈ 5.4 eV).

Hence, number of free electrons for conduction in Ge and Si are significant at room temperature, but negligibly small for C (making Carbon diamond an insulator).

WORKED NUMERICAL PROBLEM Equations 14.1, 14.2 & ρ = 1/σ

Problem: In a pure intrinsic Silicon specimen in Virtual Lab 3 at T = 300 K, an applied potential difference produces a free-electron current Ie = 1.80 mA and a hole current Ih = 0.65 mA. If the intrinsic electron concentration is ne = 1.5 × 1016 m−3, find (a) the hole concentration nh, and (b) the total current I in SI units.

1. Given: Ie = 1.80×10−3 A, Ih = 0.65×10−3 A, ne = 1.5×1016 m−3
2. Required: Hole density nh and Total current I
3. Equations: ne = nh = ni (Eq. 14.1) & I = Ie + Ih (Eq. 14.2)
4. Substitution: I = (1.80 + 0.65) × 10−3 A
5 & 6. Final Answer: (a) nh = 1.5 × 1016 m−3  |  (b) I = 2.45 × 10−3 A (or 2.45 mA)
Relatable Analogy & Real-World Connections

Analogy: The Empty Seat in a Packed Auditorium

Imagine a completely filled front row of chairs (the Valence Band at 0 K). Nobody can shift sideways because every seat is occupied.

Now, one student at Seat 1 gets excited and climbs up to the empty balcony (the Conduction Band) where they can run freely. This leaves an empty seat (Hole) at Seat 1. When a seated student at Seat 2 shifts into Seat 1, the empty seat appears to move from Seat 1 → Seat 2!

Scientific Limitation of Analogy: Unlike a passive empty chair, a hole in a crystal lattice represents an effective positive charge (+q) due to the uncompensated +4 ionic core and experiences an electrostatic force towards negative potential.

Real-Life & Engineering Applications

  • NTC Thermistors (Temperature Sensors): Because thermal energy exponentially increases ni in intrinsic semiconductors, their resistivity drops sharply with temperature—used in medical digital thermometers and EV battery thermal management.
  • Photodetectors & Solar Cells (Si & GaAs): Photons with energy hν ≥ Eg excite electrons across the band gap to generate electron-hole pairs directly from sunlight.
  • OLED Screens & Polymer Electronics: Post-1990 organic semiconductors (anthracene, polyaniline, polypyrrole) enable ultra-thin foldable smartphone displays.
